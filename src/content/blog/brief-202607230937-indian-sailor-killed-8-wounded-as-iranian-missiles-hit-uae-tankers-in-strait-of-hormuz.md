<p>One Indian crew member was killed and eight others wounded, including two Ukrainians, when Iranian cruise missiles hit two UAE-flagged oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the UAE Ministry of Defence said on Tuesday. The attack on the Mombasa and Al Bahiyah inside Omani territorial waters marks the deadliest spillover yet into commercial shipping from the conflict that began on February 28 when the U.S. and Israel launched strikes on Iran.</p>

<p>The ministry said the missiles struck the vessels in the southern lane of the strategic waterway, triggering fires that have since been brought under control. Six of the wounded are Indian nationals and two are Ukrainian. Four of the wounded were seriously injured. The dead sailor was aboard the Mombasa. The UAE condemned the “blatant attack” and said it retains “its full right to respond to this escalation.”</p>

<p>The strike comes a day after U.S. President Donald Trump announced a reinstated blockade of Iranian shipping in the Gulf, warning that U.S. forces would hit Iran again “very hard” while pledging to keep the strait open — for a fee. Trump’s move followed a weekend exchange of missile and drone attacks and throws into doubt a fragile interim U.S.-Iranian deal signed last month to halt hostilities and reopen the waterway.</p>

<p>Iran’s top joint military command dismissed any U.S. role in determining the strait’s future, saying it would not be allowed to intervene. Iran has yet to comment on Tuesday’s tanker attack. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency separately reported a vessel hit by a projectile 40 nautical miles northeast of Oman’s Qalhat, though it remained unclear if this referred to the same incident.</p>

<p>The escalating confrontation directly imperils the free flow of roughly one-fifth of the world’s daily oil traffic, which before the war exceeded 15 million barrels of fuel worth at least $1.2 billion. For energy-importing nations across the Global South, particularly India — whose nationals made up nearly all the casualties — the unchecked targeting of merchant shipping in Omani waters signals a dangerous new phase that risks disrupting vital supply chains and pushing up global fuel costs.</p>
