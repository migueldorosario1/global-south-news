<p>A United Nations expert has drawn a direct line from British colonial counterinsurgency tactics of the 1920s to the systematic sexual torture of Palestinians by Israeli forces today, arguing that the abuse is embedded in the state’s legal and security architecture as an inherited imperial method. Francesca Albanese, the UN special rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territories, told an Al Jazeera investigation that practices deployed against the Irish insurgency were exported to Mandate Palestine and “immediately received and ingrained in the Israeli system.”</p>
<p>The investigation, a companion to the documentary Bodies of Evidence: Israel’s Darkest Weapon, documents how approximately 650 former members of the Black and Tans—the British paramilitary force notorious for terror in Ireland—were redeployed in 1922 to form the British Palestine Gendarmerie. Albanese traces an institutional chain from Ireland through the suppression of the 1936 Arab Revolt, Britain’s use of sexual violence against Mau Mau detainees in Kenya, and France’s industrialised torture during the Algerian war, techniques that she says Israel inherited as a settler-colonial project. The source further states that torture in colonial and racially ordered regimes is used to inflict humiliation and erasure as a method of control.</p>
<p>Survivor testimony underscores the pattern. Abdel Latif Ghaith, later director of the prisoner-rights group Addameer, recalled witnessing, in 1969, the interrogation of Rasmea Odeh in a Jerusalem detention centre. Odeh subsequently testified to a UN committee in 1979 that she was raped with a stick, subjected to electric shocks to her genitals and mouth, and threatened that her father would be forced to rape her. Kifaya Khraim, international advocacy coordinator at the Ramallah-based Women’s Centre for Legal Aid and Counselling, said Israeli forces exploit social stigma, with many women describing inserted devices “without being able to identify that as rape or sexual violence.”</p>
<p>Former International Criminal Court judge Cuno Tarfusser stated that such sexual violence “becomes a method to fight the war,” prosecutable as torture, war crime, or crime against humanity. The report highlights how colonial counterinsurgency techniques are repurposed to subjugate indigenous populations, with Albanese emphasizing that “torture is quite common in colonial systems or racially ordered regimes … because the infliction of humiliation and erasure is seen as a practice to control.”</p>
