<p>One Indian crew member was killed and eight others wounded when Iranian cruise missiles struck two Emirati oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, the United Arab Emirates Ministry of Defence said, sharply escalating tensions in the strategic energy corridor.</p> <p>The missiles hit the tankers Mombasa and Al Bahiyah while they were transiting Omani territorial waters in the southern lane of the strait. The dead seafarer was aboard the Mombasa, the ministry said. Of the wounded, four were in serious condition; six were Indian nationals and two were Ukrainian nationals. Fires broke out on both vessels but were subsequently brought under control; the ministry said the attack caused material damage to both tankers.</p> <p>The UAE condemned what it called a “blatant attack” and said it retained “its full right to respond to this escalation.” The ministry added that it remained fully prepared to deal with any threats and was taking all necessary measures to respond firmly to any attempts to undermine the country’s security and stability. Iran has not commented on the incident.</p> <p>The strike is the latest in a widening confrontation that began on February 28 with joint US-Israeli military action against Iran. On Monday, President Donald Trump said the US was reinstating a blockade of Iranian shipping and would guarantee passage through Hormuz “for a fee,” warning of further strikes. Iran’s military command responded that Washington had no role in determining the waterway’s future. An interim US-Iranian agreement reached last month to reopen the strait and halt hostilities now appears in question.</p> <p>For the Global South, the attack underscores the disproportionate human toll on seafarers from developing nations who crew much of the world’s merchant fleet. Before the conflict, roughly one-fifth of global oil and gas traffic—over 15 million barrels a day worth at least $1.2bn—passed through Hormuz, and any prolonged disruption threatens energy security and economic stability across emerging economies that rely on affordable fuel imports.</p>
