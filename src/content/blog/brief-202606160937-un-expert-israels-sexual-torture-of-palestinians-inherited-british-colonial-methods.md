<p>UN Special Rapporteur Francesca Albanese has stated that Israel's systematic use of sexual violence against Palestinian detainees is a direct inheritance of British colonial counterinsurgency methods, describing torture as a constant feature of Israeli state practice “since the very beginning of its existence.” The assessment, delivered in Al Jazeera's documentary *Bodies of Evidence: Israel's Darkest Weapon*, traces a chain of institutional knowledge transfer stretching from Ireland through Mandate Palestine to present-day detention facilities.</p>
<p>The genealogy of abuse is starkly documented. Following the Irish War of Independence, approximately 650 former members of the notorious Black and Tans paramilitary force were redeployed to Mandate Palestine in 1922 to form the British Palestine Gendarmerie. They brought with them interrogation techniques refined against the Irish insurgency, alongside emergency regulations that Israel subsequently incorporated wholesale into its own legal code. Albanese emphasised that these laws were not adapted or modernised but “immediately received and ingrained in the Israeli system,” creating a durable legal architecture that has licensed administrative detention and custodial violence for decades.</p>
<p>Kifaya Khraim, international advocacy coordinator at the Women's Centre for Legal Aid and Counselling (WCLAC), noted that Israeli forces deliberately exploit the intense social stigma surrounding sexual violence, knowing that many victims struggle to identify or report such violations. Former International Criminal Court judge Cuno Tarfusser characterised such acts as a calculated war method rather than incidental abuse, stating that “sexual violence becomes more than crime … sexual crimes become a method to fight the war.” The documentary situates Israel's practices within a wider colonial lineage that includes French tactics in Algeria and apartheid South Africa's systematised brutality, without asserting direct doctrinal absorption.</p>
<p>Survivor testimony underscores the continuity. Rasmea Odeh described to a UN committee in 1979 being raped with a stick and subjected to genital electrocution in a Jerusalem interrogation room a decade earlier. Abdel Latif Ghaith, later director of Addameer, witnessed her ordeal from an adjacent cell. Khraim explained that Israeli forces understand the social consequences and weaponize that stigma. For observers across the Global South, the findings expose how colonial-era legal structures and direct personnel transfers continue to license state-perpetrated torture as an instrument of collective erasure.</p>
