<p>Francesca Albanese, UN Special Rapporteur on the Occupied Palestinian Territories, has documented how Israel’s systemic use of sexual torture against Palestinians since 1948 directly inherits and operationalises colonial counterinsurgency methods pioneered by Britain in Ireland and Kenya and refined by France in Algeria. Speaking to Al Jazeera for the documentary *Bodies of Evidence*, Albanese traces a continuous institutional lineage—not metaphorical but material—linking personnel, legal frameworks, and violent practices across imperial regimes.</p>
<p>Archival evidence confirms that approximately 650 former British Black and Tans were redeployed to Mandate Palestine in April 1922 to form the Palestine Gendarmerie. Their tactics—including sexualised humiliation, forced nudity, and coercive interrogation—were codified in British emergency regulations later adopted wholesale into Israeli law. As Albanese notes, these provisions were “immediately received and ingrained”, enabling administrative detention and impunity for security forces acting “in good faith”.</p>
<p>Rasmea Odeh, detained in Jerusalem in 1969, described rape with a stick, electric shocks to her genitals, and threats to force her father to assault her—testimony she delivered before a UN committee in Geneva in 1979. Abdel Latif Ghaith of Addameer, who witnessed parts of her ordeal, stresses such abuse was neither isolated nor novel. Kifaya Khraim of the Women’s Centre for Legal Aid and Counselling (WCLAC) adds that Israeli forces deliberately exploit social stigma, deploying devices and acts designed to evade victims’ own recognition as sexual violence.</p>
<p>The 1956 Protocol of Sèvres alliance between Israel, Britain, and France further cemented doctrinal transfer, particularly France’s institutionalisation of torture during the Algerian War. As Albanese observes, “torture is quite common in colonial systems or racially ordered regimes because the infliction of humiliation and erasure is seen as a practice to control.” For Global South states, this lineage exposes how colonial legal and violent architectures persist—not as relics, but as functioning instruments undermining accountability and the universality of international humanitarian law.</p>
