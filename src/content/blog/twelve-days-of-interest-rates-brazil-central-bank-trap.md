**By Ricardo Lodi Ribeiro**
*Tenured Professor of Financial Law at UERJ, former Rector of UERJ, and author of "Budget for the Rich"*

Brazil spends the entire year debating its fiscal target. Ministries have their appropriations blocked, public works are halted, civil service exams are postponed, and funding for public hospitals and universities is squeezed. Governments have fallen in the name of fiscal responsibility. All of this stems from a premise presented as self-evident and, for that very reason, almost never subjected to open debate: the public accounts must be balanced.

Let us admit, for the sake of argument, this premise in all its breadth. Suppose that the public deficit is, in itself, an evil and that debt growth represents a threat to future generations. Even so, an uncomfortable question remains: does the primary result target, around which much of Brazilian fiscal policy is organized, effectively serve to produce this balance?

The answer is no. And a single number suffices to realize the dimension of the problem.

The primary surplus target set for 2026 is R$ 34 billion (around $6.2 billion USD). The interest bill on public debt, at last year's level, consumes just under R$ 3 billion per day. The entire target, therefore, is equivalent to **twelve days of interest**.

Put another way, it becomes even more eloquent. Last year, the nominal deficit — the one that includes interest and reveals the true, net result of public accounts — reached R$ 1.06 trillion. Of this total, the primary result accounted for about five percent. Interest accounted for the other ninety-five percent.

The target set for 2026 corresponds to roughly three percent of this financial hole. Even if it is fully met, with all the budget freezes, blockages, and social sacrifices that its compliance demands, more than ninety percent of the problem will remain exactly where it is.

Twelve days. That is the size of the effort around which Brazilian fiscal life is organized. The entire apparatus of budget impoundments, end-of-year fiscal crises, postponed investments, and sacrificed public policies serve to achieve a magnitude that financial expenditure consumes in less than two weeks.

Here appears the contradiction that defenders of selective austerity rarely face. If the public deficit produces all the evils attributed to it, why does concern concentrate precisely on the primary result, which represents such a small portion of the total deficit? Why does the nominal deficit, nearly twenty times larger, not spark the same obsession?

## The Legal Shielding of Debt Service

The answer lies not just in economics. It lies in Financial Law.

The Fiscal Responsibility Law (LRF) requires that the annual fiscal guidelines law establish targets for both primary and nominal results. The second includes interest and, therefore, measures what is commonly called the effective result of public accounts. Both targets are legally prescribed. But only one of them produces concrete consequences for budget execution.

The mechanism is embedded in Article 9 of the LRF itself. Its caput mandates spending limits when revenue realization threatens compliance with primary or nominal targets. However, paragraph 2 excludes certain expenses from these limits — including those dedicated to debt service payments.

It is worth re-reading the legal architecture. The law commands expenditure cuts to ensure compliance with the nominal target, while simultaneously shielding from cuts the exact item that most directly affects that result. The adjustment falls entirely on what can be impounded: public investments and social policies, including health and education.

This is neither an economic fatality nor a neutral market imposition. It is a normative choice. Constitutional Amendment No. 95 in 2016 followed the same logic by capping primary expenses without touching debt service. The 2023 fiscal framework preserved this asymmetry: failure to meet the primary target triggers restrictions on expense growth; failure to meet the nominal target triggers no equivalent mechanism.

This design reveals what we call the **legal shielding of debt service**. In a republican budget, competing interests for public resources should be subjected to transparent criteria of priority and democratic legitimacy. When creditor interests are legally placed beyond the reach of adjustment mechanisms that squeeze all other expenses, technical neutrality merely disguises a clear distributional choice.

## Maximum Social Sacrifice, Marginal Fiscal Effect

One might object that interest rates are fundamentally set by monetary policy rather than the budget. That is true. But it does not follow that they should be legally untouchable. On the contrary: precisely because such a large share of financial spending is driven by decisions made outside the budgetary process, its fiscal immunity should be subject to greater democratic scrutiny, not less.

Brazil's historical experience illustrates the mistake. During the first Lula administration, the country recorded the largest primary surpluses in its recent history. It was also a period of extremely high interest payments. The nominal result remained worse than in the preceding four-year period. In 2016, the opposite occurred: the primary result deteriorated while the nominal result improved.

An indicator that worsens while the phenomenon it is supposed to represent improves cannot be accepted as a sufficient measure of fiscal health. It is measuring something else.

And indeed it is. The primary result reveals how much the State collects above what it spends on public policies before remunerating debt holders. Therefore, the primary target is not merely a rule of balance; it is also a rule of distribution. By ensuring that fiscal sacrifice falls on primary expenses while debt service remains protected, an order of priority is established between financial creditors and citizens.

At this point, austerity ceases to be merely an economic doctrine and transforms into a legal architecture for resource distribution. The budget, which should express democratic choices about rights fulfillment and economic development, operates under a pre-defined hierarchy where certain public expenses can be squeezed while others remain permanently shielded.

Returning to the initial question: if the public deficit is the core problem, there is no reason for fiscal debate to focus almost exclusively on its smallest component. Silence regarding the nominal deficit reveals that the core of the controversy was never just the size of the deficit, but who should bear the cost of addressing it.

I do not argue that the primary result is irrelevant to the debt trajectory. I argue something simpler: the target, as currently designed, costs a great deal and solves very little. Maximum social sacrifice, marginal fiscal effect.

That is why the debate is misformulated. The issue is not merely whether the target should be a quarter or half a point of GDP, or which expenses can be excluded from the calculation. We must ask why the legal system transforms the primary target into an effective instrument for compressing public policies, while the nominal target, provided for by the very same law, remains devoid of equivalent tools for enforcement.

Until this asymmetry is confronted, we will continue discussing fiscal responsibility while looking at only a small fraction of the problem — and sacrificing public policies to fulfill a target that equals barely twelve days of interest.
