<p>Vladimir Arreaga, director of Forecasting at Ecuador’s national meteorological agency, warned Thursday that the El Niño climate phenomenon will likely arrive by July or August, with an estimated probability exceeding 80 percent. The assessment, based on equatorial Pacific monitoring, signals a mounting threat to food security and climate risk management across the region.</p>
<p>“We already have signals in the equatorial Pacific Ocean,” Arreaga said at a press event in Quito. He pointed to consolidated warm water masses and weakening winds as conditions consistently linked to the formation of the event. Should the oceanic anomaly couple with atmospheric patterns, Arreaga added, the phenomenon could sharply intensify rainfall during the upcoming winter season along Ecuador’s coast because of elevated sea surface temperatures. By contrast, parts of the Amazon basin could face reduced precipitation, echoing the uneven impact observed in earlier El Niño episodes.</p>
<p>The warning coincided with the launch of a new weather information dissemination system spearheaded by the Ministry of Environment and Energy, the national meteorological institute Inamhi, and the United Nations World Food Programme (WFP). The initiative, backed by close to $2 million in WFP investment, has financed roughly 30 local climate stations delivering real-time data, primarily in the coastal province of Manabí and the northern Andean province of Imbabura.</p>
<p>Mateo Perrone, the WFP’s representative in Ecuador, said the stations now provide ongoing, updated information on territorial conditions. The improved bulletins, built around clearer formats and digital channels, are designed to help authorities, farmers and vulnerable populations anticipate weather-related disruptions and make timely decisions. As El Niño approaches, officials stressed that accurate, accessible forecasting will be essential to safeguarding livelihoods in a country where past events have repeatedly battered agriculture and critical infrastructure.</p>
