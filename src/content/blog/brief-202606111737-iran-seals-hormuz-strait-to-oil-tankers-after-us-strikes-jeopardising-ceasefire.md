<p>Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps closed the Strait of Hormuz to all oil tankers and commercial ships on 11 June, declaring it would fire on any vessel attempting passage. The order, announced via state media, answered a new wave of US airstrikes that President Donald Trump authorised and Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth confirmed were hitting “key facilities” in Iran.</p>
<p>The closure escalates the deadliest exchanges since an April ceasefire and compounds a partial blockade in force since February’s US-Israeli strikes. The strait normally carries 20 percent of global oil and liquefied natural gas, and analysts warned that the renewed disruption risks sustained high energy costs for Global South economies heavily dependent on imported fuel.</p>
<p>US Central Command described its latest strikes as “self-defense,” citing earlier attacks on US forces and commercial ships including the downing of a US Apache helicopter. Iran’s IRGC subsequently launched drone and missile attacks on the US Fifth Fleet in Bahrain and an airbase in Jordan. Foad Izadi, a professor at the University of Tehran, told Al Jazeera that Iran wants to keep control of the Strait of Hormuz. “Iran will pay whatever price that comes with that because that gives you leverage,” he said.</p>
<p>Brussels-based military analyst Elijah Magnier cautioned that each side’s belief it can manage the tit-for-tat cycle is “dangerous” and risks miscalculation. Samir Puri, a senior fellow at the International Institute for Strategic Studies, added that the pattern points to a situation that will not “de-escalate any time soon”. Both warned that the spiral could collapse Pakistan-mediated peace talks, which remain deadlocked over whether Lebanon must be included in any final settlement. Tehran declared the ceasefire “useless” after the latest strikes, while Washington insisted military pressure aims to force a permanent deal.</p>
