<p>Bolivian President Paz has sent legislation to congress to regulate a state of exception, as nationwide protests demanding his resignation enter their 35th day, deepening critical shortages of fuel, medical supplies and food.</p><p>Paz announced on Wednesday that the bill would “strengthen” the armed forces in what he termed a “humanitarian action,” amid road blockades led by the Central Obrera Boliviana (COB), supporters of former president Evo Morales, neighborhood federations, miners, farmers, teachers and transport workers. The protesters have severed key highways, paralysing supply chains to urban centres.</p><p>Vice President Édman Lara, who has publicly opposed Paz’s government, rejected the proposed regulation and urged a negotiated settlement. Lara offered the mediation of his office, the Catholic Church and the Human Rights Ombudsman to revive government-protest talks that have so far failed to yield results.</p><p>In La Paz and El Alto, the month-long blockades have choked off oxygen for hospitals, medicines and basic foodstuffs, with prices of scarce goods doubling or tripling. Lawmaker Patiño, from a dissident faction of the Christian Democratic Party that helped bring Paz to power, joined a hunger strike by legislators to protest the stalemate. At the Ombudsman’s office, three housewives have been on hunger strike for 14 days, surviving only on coca leaves, and objecting to the repeal of a law that barred immunity for security forces during protest repression.</p>
