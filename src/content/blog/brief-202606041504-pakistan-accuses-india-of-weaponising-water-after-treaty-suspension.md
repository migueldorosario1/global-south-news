<p>ISLAMABAD — Pakistan accused India on June 4, 2026 of weaponising shared water resources after New Delhi announced two projects on the Chenab River, escalating tensions between the nuclear-armed neighbours over a landmark treaty that has governed cross-border rivers for decades.</p>
<p>Foreign Ministry spokesperson Tahir Andrabi said a proposed tunnel to divert water from the Chenab to the Beas basin, and sediment removal works at the Salal Power Station, violated the Indus Waters Treaty and threatened Pakistan’s economy and regional stability. “These projects confirm that India seems to weaponise water,” Andrabi told reporters. “This carries dangerous implications not only for Pakistan’s economy but also for regional stability and international peace and security.”</p>
<p>India suspended the 1960 pact in 2025 following a deadly attack on tourists in Indian-administered Kashmir, which New Delhi blamed on Pakistan-backed militants. Islamabad denied the charge, but the suspension triggered intense cross-border exchanges that left nearly 70 people dead. Andrabi insisted the treaty remains binding and said Pakistan would “retain all options necessary” to safeguard its rights, without elaborating.</p>
<p>India’s Power Ministry confirmed in January it was undertaking sediment removal “following the termination” of the treaty. In May, state-owned NHPC issued a tender for the Chenab diversion project. India’s Foreign Ministry has maintained it is within its rights to proceed, and last month dismissed a Hague-based arbitration court ruling that Pakistan said supported its position. “India’s decision to hold the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance remains in force,” the ministry said.</p>
<p>The dispute highlights how water security is emerging as a critical flashpoint, with climate change and population growth straining resources in both agriculture-dependent economies.</p>
